Easing
functions.

An easing function reshapes time. It takes progress t from 0 to 1 and returns how far along the motion should be, so an animation can start slowly, overshoot, or bounce instead of moving at a constant speed. Pick a curve, change its settings, watch it drive real animations, and copy the code.

HISTORY

        
Where easing came from

        
    
          
  1. 1930sDisney animators develop the rule of slow in and slow out: drawings bunch up near key poses, so motion gathers speed and settles, the way real objects do.
    2. 
          
  2. 1960sPierre Bézier (Renault) and Paul de Casteljau (Citroën) independently develop the curves that later become CSS's cubic-bezier().
    3. 
          
  3. 1981Ollie Johnston and Frank Thomas publish The Illusion of Life, setting out Disney's 12 principles of animation. Slow in and slow out is one of them, and anticipation (the wind-up that the Back easing imitates) is another.
    4. 
          
  4. ~2001Robert Penner publishes his easing equations for Flash's ActionScript: quad, cubic, quart, quint, sine, expo, circ, back, elastic and bounce, each in in/out/in-out forms. He released them under a permissive open-source licence, and almost every easing library since descends from them. He covered them in his 2002 book on Flash MX programming.
    5. 
          
  5. 2007Apple's WebKit team proposes CSS transitions and animations, with timing functions defined as cubic Bézier curves (ease, ease-in, ease-out, ease-in-out).
    6. 
          
  6. 2008George McGinley Smith's jQuery Easing plugin ports Penner's equations to JavaScript, bringing names like easeOutBounce to a generation of web developers.
    7. 
          
  7. 2013iOS 7 adds spring-based animations to UIKit. Physics-based springs become a staple of mobile and later web UI (React Spring, Framer Motion, SwiftUI).
    8. 
          
  8. 2023Browsers ship CSS linear(), which accepts a list of points. For the first time, bounce and elastic curves can be approximated in pure CSS.
    9. 
        

        
    
          
  • Why "in" and "out"? The name says where the easing (the slowness) is. Ease-in starts slowly and accelerates; ease-out starts fast and decelerates; in-out does both.
    • 
          
  • One trick builds them all: out(t) = 1 − in(1 − t). Any ease-out is its ease-in flipped and rotated, and in-out runs a half-size ease-in followed by a half-size ease-out.
    • 
          
  • A popular reference site for these curves, easings.net, was made by Andrey Sitnik and Ivan Solovev.
